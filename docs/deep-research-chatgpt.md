## Why Vim still matters

The current trend is not “everyone is moving to raw Vim.” The trend is that **Vim has become a language layer** that lives inside other environments. That is visible in three places at once: editor surveys, plugin install counts, and modern learning products. Stack Overflow’s 2025 survey shows Vim and Neovim still used by professionals in nontrivial numbers, even while VS Code dominates overall editor usage. The Marketplace install counts show that millions of people want Vim motions inside VS Code specifically, not necessarily a separate editor identity. And newer products such as VimHero and VimKata are explicitly teaching Vim as a practical, composable editing language rather than as a romantic terminal lifestyle choice. citeturn2view1turn2view0turn6view0turn6view1turn35view0turn38view0

This matters for your prognosis. The most durable part of Vim is not “`.vimrc` wizardry”; it is the small grammar of **motions, operators, text objects, repeat, search, and marks**. That grammar remains useful whether the surrounding environment is Vim, Neovim, VS Code, a browser extension, or a shell’s vi mode. In the VS Code ecosystem, **VSCodeVim** supports remappings, visual-mode keybindings, text-object plugins, surround/commentary-style features, and multi-cursor mode, while **VSCode Neovim** positions itself as “feature-complete Vim integration” except for insert-mode handling and some Neovim UI plugins, and it integrates with VS Code’s LSP, snippets, and multi-cursor features. That is exactly the pattern you described: modal editing as a fast text-control substrate embedded inside a modern editor rather than a replacement for the editor. citeturn23view1turn24view0turn24view1turn24view2

LLMs do change the value proposition, but mostly by **raising the bar for what your Vim training should optimize for**. If a user wants a broad semantic refactor across files, an LLM or a purpose-built script often becomes attractive. But if they want to do “change inside quotes,” “delete next argument,” “fix these five local lines,” “jump by search and repeat,” or “do a deterministic structural edit without leaving flow,” Vim remains relevant precisely because there is no setup latency and no handoff cost. The stronger the AI tooling gets, the more your product should emphasize **sub-minute deterministic edits** and **automation building blocks** like macros, repeat, text objects, registers, search, and efficient multi-step transformations. The survey data showing continued Vim/Neovim use among AI-using professionals supports that interpretation. citeturn2view0turn23view1turn24view1

My prognosis is therefore fairly stable: **Vim fluency will probably remain relevant for at least the next several years**, but increasingly as a **specialized productivity skill for power users**, not as a universal editor default. That is actually good for product opportunity. Mature universal markets are crowded; specialized markets with persistent pain and weak training products are often better solo-business markets. citeturn2view0turn6view0turn6view1

Its coverage is better than many people remember. The official list includes not just basics like `hjkl`, `w`, `b`, `e`, `x`, `dd`, `dw`, `i`, `a`, and `o`, but also **search** (`/`, `?`, `*`, `#`, `n`, `N`), **text objects**, **registers**, **marks**, counts, repeat (`.`), and some file/buffer commands. That means it does cover the **core compositional grammar** that actually makes Vim valuable for real editing. ## What mobile could and could not teach

Your mobile idea is more viable than it might seem, but only if you position it correctly. The right frame is **mobile as a companion for command grammar, recognition, decision speed, and recall**, not **mobile as a full substitute for physical-keyboard muscle memory**. Convenient practice matters. Aalto/Cambridge/ETH researchers measured mobile typing with **37,370 volunteers** and found average mobile typing around **36.2 WPM** with **2.3% uncorrected errors**, while two-thumb typists averaged about **38 WPM**, roughly **25% slower** than physical keyboards in their comparisons. At the same time, phone typing is no longer disastrously slow; it is simply a different modality. citeturn15search0turn15search4

The transfer question is where the nuance matters. Research on virtual keyboards consistently finds that the lack of tactile feedback changes behavior: a 2014 ergonomics study comparing virtual and conventional keyboards found productivity differences tied to the no-travel, no-tactile-feedback condition, and later work notes that unlike physical keyboards, virtual keyboards typically require **more frequent eye movements** because users cannot depend as fully on long-term muscle memory. Aalto’s mobile typing research group also says there is **some cross-device relation** because both systems share QWERTY knowledge; they note that fast touchscreen typists also tend to perform well on physical keyboards. So the best evidence-supported answer is: **yes, some transfer exists, but it is partial**. The abstract command sequences—like `ciw`, `f,`, `dt"`, `3dd`, `ggVG`, or `:%s/.../.../g`—can be learned and recalled on mobile. The tactile, eyes-free, immediate execution of those sequences on a real keyboard will still need real-keyboard practice. citeturn14search1turn16search23turn16search18

Interestingly, at least one new mobile product in this space has converged on exactly that positioning. VimKata explicitly says: **“Your physical keyboard builds the muscle memory. Learn in the app, apply in your editor.”** It describes itself as a companion that teaches the mental model, with the phone teaching the grammar and the terminal teaching the typing. That is a strong signal that the market is already discovering the same product truth you intuited. And there is visible mobile demand: VimKata is live on Android and iOS in 2026, while the broader “Vim Master” app has already reached **100K+ downloads** on Google Play. citeturn38view0turn38view1turn38view2

So should you keep the 2D pixel-art style? You could, but I would not treat that as the key decision. The market now appears bifurcated. Vim Adventures proves that a playful 2D world can be memorable and motivating. Newer products like VimHero and VimKata are moving toward **cleaner, faster, lesson-centric UIs** with real-code exercises, generated drills, and less navigational overhead. For a mobile-first product, that likely argues for a **hybrid**: keep some identity and charm—pixel art, map progression, NPCs, world themes—while making the **practice loop extremely short**. On a commute, users do not want to walk around a maze for three minutes before they get to `ci"` drills. The world should be flavor, progression, and retention scaffolding; the core loop should be **one exercise, one insight, one retry**. citeturn31search1turn35view0turn38view0

## Differentiation, ML potential, and build cost

Your best differentiator is not “another Vim game.” It is a product that is explicitly about **practical edit automation and mechanical retention for modern editor workflows**. I would build around the following product thesis: **teach the small set of Vim operations that save time every week inside VS Code and terminals, then adapt practice to what the user forgets**. That means prioritizing motions, operators, text objects, repeat, search, substitutions, macros, visual mode, and editor-embedded workflows before spending energy on a large fantasy overworld or advanced standalone-editor configuration. The fact that Vim Adventures underweights visual mode, regex/search-replace, and macros in the core path creates room here, and the fact that VS Code integrations emphasize multi-cursor, text objects, and native-editor interop makes the opportunity more specific, not less. citeturn31search2turn26view2turn23view1turn24view1

A strong modern alternative could differentiate along four axes at once. First, **editor relevance**: make it explicitly “for VS Code / Neovim users,” with lessons that reflect real tasks like changing arguments, editing quoted strings, repeating search-driven fixes, and bridging into multi-cursor or IDE actions. Second, **adaptive practice**: track per-skill proficiency, hesitations, failed attempts, extra keystrokes, hint usage, and decay over time, then schedule the next lesson around weakness and forgetting. Third, **mobile companion mode**: position mobile as concept and recall rehearsal, synced with desktop practice for final transfer. Fourth, **lifetime usefulness**: avoid the current market pain around expiring access by offering lifetime or at least “keep your drills forever” pricing. Public sentiment repeatedly indicates that access model is one of the weakest points in the incumbent. citeturn22search4turn13search2turn13search4

Yes, this can absolutely become a real data-science and ML project. Educational data mining already has fitting tools for exactly this kind of product. Work on **knowledge tracing** models tracks how a learner’s mastery evolves over time; recent reviews describe KT as modeling learner knowledge from sequential interactions, and game-specific work such as **GameDKT** applies deep knowledge tracing directly to educational games. There is also evidence that **Bayesian Knowledge Tracing**-based explainable recommenders can improve productive engagement and growth in e-learning settings, and Google’s work on **federated learning for mobile keyboard prediction** demonstrates the feasibility of privacy-preserving on-device learning for keyboard-like behavior. In practice, that means you could build a portfolio-worthy stack around: interpretable mastery estimation with BKT, richer sequential modeling with modern KT methods, recommendation policies for “next best drill,” and privacy-preserving local adaptation for mobile telemetry. citeturn27search9turn27search6turn28view2turn28view1

There is also a credible motor-learning angle. Evidence on spacing for motor skills is mixed rather than simple, but research does show that **interleaved practice** can improve **retention and transfer** for implicit sequence learning even when blocked practice feels easier during training. That is highly relevant to a Vim trainer. Users should not just grind `w` fifty times in a row. They should revisit mixed patterns—`ciw`, `dt,`, `f"`, `/search + n`, `3dd`, visual-mode transformations, macro snippets—in a way that forces retrieval and flexible transfer. Even the newer debate around spacing in motor learning points to an important design lesson: you should not oversell “scientific spaced repetition” in a simplistic way, but you absolutely can build a serious system around **adaptive interleaving, retention checks, and weak-skill resurfacing**. citeturn30search2turn30search11turn29view1

On AI-generated content, my recommendation is: use LLMs for **theme, narrative, world generation, NPC dialogue, scenario variety, and code-context diversification**, but do **not** rely on LLMs alone to define correct solutions. A Vim trainer needs exact answer validity and, ideally, near-optimality. The reliable approach is to generate or transform tasks **symbolically**, validate them against an actual editor backend or parser, and use LLMs only where some fuzziness is acceptable. For example, you can let LLMs generate “quests” (“rename the obvious typo,” “extract the next argument,” “clean this repeated suffix”), but the underlying input/output buffer state and accepted solutions should be verified programmatically. That keeps the product trustworthy and turns the ML layer into a strength instead of a source of support tickets. The current crop of products moving toward real-code challenges, generated exercises, and tracked proficiency suggests the market will reward this seriousness. citeturn35view0turn38view0
